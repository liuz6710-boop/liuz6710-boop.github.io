— THE QUESTION

Is there a business here, or just a request?

Merchants selling on TikTok Shop, Shopee and Lazada have a different shape of demand from the platform's traditional customers: smaller orders, far more of them, tighter delivery promises, and returns as a normal part of the flow — not an exception. The warehouse could physically handle that work. The question was whether it was worth building the product around it.

Engineering capacity was already spoken for. Anything new had to displace something else, which meant the case had to be made in numbers, not enthusiasm.

— THE ANALYSIS

Start from behaviour, not from the pitch.

Merchant behaviour

Order profiles, volumes and seasonality from the marketplace channels — what these merchants actually ship, how often, and in what sizes.

Fit against operations

Which parts of the existing warehouse flow absorb this demand unchanged, and which parts break — pick profiles, packing, returns handling.

Revenue model

Volume assumptions mapped onto the platform's charging model, extended over a five-year horizon, with the assumptions written down so they could be challenged.

Cost of building it

What the platform would need — order intake, fulfilment workflow, returns — and what else would slip if this went first.

— THE CASE

S$1.5M+ over five years, with the assumptions on the table.

I presented the case to leadership as a decision, not a demo: here is the opportunity, here is what we believe and why, here is what it costs, and here is what we would not do instead. The numbers held up under questioning because the assumptions were explicit rather than buried in a spreadsheet.

  • Sized at S$1.5M+ in projected revenue over five years, built up from merchant order behaviour rather than a top-down market estimate.
  • Won prioritisation against competing initiatives — the initiative was taken into scope on the strength of the case.
  • Scoped into product requirements — order intake from marketplace channels, fulfilment workflow, and the integration surface the merchants' systems needed.

— WHAT I TOOK FROM IT

The hardest product skill is saying what it's worth.

Building the thing is rarely the constraint. Deciding what deserves to be built — and being able to defend that in front of people who are weighing it against their own priorities — is the part that changes what a team spends its year on. This is the piece of the job I want more of, not less.
